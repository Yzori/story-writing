Where do the mechanics live?

Three concepts for the core play surface. Same locked spec, same scene, same five states — three different answers to the only question that decides whether this feels calm or messy. Click the states under each concept.

One beat of play

The Director writes. Naming @Kael hands him the pen. He writes one passage; the pen returns.

Two mechanics

The 2d6 dice and the table's vote (with the audience leaning in). Nothing else ships in v1.

Waiting is reading

The book, plus "… is writing." No panels, no queues, no cockpit.

Four chairs

Director · players · the audience beyond the light. Sessions still compile into chapters.

I

Set in Ink

The mechanics live in the type itself. There is one page and nothing else — no seats, no station, no margin. Presence is a signature line. The pass is a written name. The dice and the vote are the only two moments the page stops being pure story, and they happen as things printed on it.

Why it might win

  • The purest expression of the thesis: if it can't be a sentence on the page, it doesn't exist.
  • Zero furniture to re-clutter later — the spec is enforced by the layout itself.
  • Collapses to mobile with no design work: it's already a single column of type.
  • The ink-identity system (already built) does all the presence work color alone can do.

Where it strains

  • Quietest of the three — the drama budget must be spent entirely on the dice cast and the vote, or it reads as a doc.
  • Presence is abstract: "who's here" is a signature line, not faces.
  • @-passing needs a good mention picker or it becomes typing friction.
II

The Open Book

The mechanics live on the live page only. You sit inside an open spread: the verso is history — settled, set type, untouchable. The recto is now — the forming paragraph, the dice, the vote. When a mechanic resolves, it settles into the verso as one printed line. Past and play are divided by the gutter.

Why it might win

  • The strongest metaphor of the three: the gutter is the mechanics-quarantine rule, made visible.
  • Flatters the output — you watch a real book accrete while you play; compile-to-chapter feels inevitable.
  • "Now writing —" as a running head is the calmest possible turn signal.
  • Spectators get the best seat in the house: a book that writes itself.

Where it strains

  • Half the screen is history you rarely re-read — expensive real estate during play.
  • The writing column is narrow on laptops.
  • On mobile the verso disappears and it quietly becomes Concept I — which asks whether the spread earns its complexity.
III

The Table

The mechanics live around the page, on the table. The room is radial: one lit page at the center, seats at the table's edge, the audience as faint points beyond the light. The pen visibly travels when a name is written. Dice are cast onto the wood, votes are slips laid beside the page — the page itself stays pure story.

Why it might win

  • Keeps the thing you already love — seats as presence — and promotes it to the whole concept.
  • Most theatrical @ pass: a physical pen crossing the table is a moment every table will feel.
  • Cleanest quarantine: mechanics literally never print on the story page.
  • The audience "beyond the light" has a natural home at the table's edge.

Where it strains

  • It's a room again — and rooms invite furniture. This is the layout that got re-cluttered twice.
  • The page shrinks to make space for wood that is empty 90% of the time.
  • Radial layouts fight mobile; the table collapses to a strip and the concept mostly evaporates.
IV

Live Ink — the ingredient that makes it alive

Every concept above still reads as "a book" for one reason: we only ever show finished text. A book is what the story leaves behind. The thing nobody has built — not Storium, not any VTT — is watching a story be written: the ink flowing, the hesitations, the crossed-out first attempt, the room holding its breath at the dice. The reader of a book sees what was written. The table sees the writing. This layer sits on top of Concept I; the demo below plays one live minute.

The moment plays itself once. Watch the ink arrive wet and dry on the page; watch the dark lean in.

The three layers

  • Live ink — words arrive as they're written: wet, glowing faintly, drying into the page. Revisions stay as struck-through ghosts until the passage is sealed. Writing becomes performance; presence becomes visible (the thing whose absence killed Storium).
  • The felt audience — the dark responds: a glimmer when someone leans in to read, a stir when the table gasps, the count breathing at the page's foot. Writers feel watched the way actors do.
  • Shared ritual time — the dice and the vote happen to everyone's page at the same instant: the room dims, the table holds, the result stamps, and one set line joins the story. Synchronized moments, not an async feed.

Honest costs

  • True keystroke streaming needs a realtime channel (we poll today). But there's a day-one version with zero infra change: commit → the ink replays. Text always arrives as flowing ink even if written privately — the page never receives dead text.
  • Stage fright is real: default to live, offer "write behind the hand" (private drafting, ink replays on commit). Both look identical to the audience.
  • Restraint required — the glimmers and stirs must stay near-subliminal or we've rebuilt the clutter as particles.
V

The House — the audience is the light

When a table plays in public, the session is a show — and a show has a house. The law that keeps it honest: the audience's money never buys the story; it buys light. The table's seals decide what happens — gold only decorates, warms, and remembers. Free viewers are the dark: real, felt, counted. Paying viewers become light the whole room can see — a gilded line, a named seat at the rim, a brighter candle. No shop chrome ever enters the room.

Tickets

Regular nights are free. Premieres and finales are ticketed — the house pays in ink to be in the room while it happens. The replay joins the book afterward.

Front-row seats

A named seat at the rim of the light — per session or monthly. Your reactions glow brighter; the cast can see who came back.

Tips on a line

Applause that pays. Tip a passage: it shimmers for the whole room, the writer's well refills, and the gilding survives into the published chapter.

The pot

Sweeten a crossroads. The pot hangs on the question, never an answer — it splits across the cast when the ink is set. Weightless by design.

Subscriptions

The Circle, extended to tables: early chapters, a credit in the playbill, a reserved seat. Or champion one character and back their writer directly.

After the show

Every session mints a free, shareable playbill — cast, title, the most-gilded lines. The compiled chapter then lives in the normal book economy.

Design laws

  • Gold never votes. Outcomes belong to the table's seals; audience influence is light and warmth, not weight.
  • One gesture, one grammar. Every paid act is "give gold, make light" — no separate features to learn. (This replaces the three bolted-on pay features we deleted: sparks, house-forks, amplifications.)
  • The dark is a real seat. Free viewers are counted, felt, and can react — generosity is the upsell, never the paywall.
  • The split is printed in the program. Even split across the cast by default, Director-adjustable, visible to the house.

Honest risks

  • Gilding can tilt writers toward crowd-pleasing — consider revealing gild totals only when the book closes, so the applause lands after the choice, not before it.
  • Ticketed doors need a full room to feel like an event — gate this behind tables that have proven an audience, or empty premieres will embarrass everyone.
  • Public rooms need the boring safety rail: Director controls the door, mutes the dark, reports work everywhere.
  • Platform cut on every flow is where Quiloria earns — set it once, print it once, never surface it in the room.
VI

The Stranger — the house's chair at the table

Opt-in when the story is made: one chair at the table is left empty, and the dark sits in it. The Stranger is a full character in the book — hooded, recurring, written in moon-silver ink — whose every action is chosen by the whole house. The Director stages its moments by writing @Stranger; the house votes; the chosen deed is set in the dark's ink. Even the Director doesn't know what it will do — the story gains a wild element, and the audience gains the one thing no show has ever given them: they are a character in the book. Controlled by presence, never by gold — the law of the House holds.

You are the audience. The Director has summoned the Stranger — cast your vote and watch the dark decide. (The house may outvote you.)

How it plays

  • Summoned like anyone else: the Director writes @Stranger and the house takes over — the same pass grammar, pointed at the dark.
  • Vote on deeds, ink by a hand: the house picks the intent; the Director (or a player the Director taps) sets it in the Stranger's silver — pace stays live, sentences stay good. Slow tables can open it to written submissions instead.
  • The dice choose a hand: when the Stranger must roll, one random watcher is handed the bones for the whole house — a lottery of glory.
  • It persists. The Stranger recurs across sessions; the house returns to see their character again. Retention is the point.
  • Cheap to build: it's the crossroads machinery (rounds, submissions, votes) wearing a hood — one system, two skins.

Design decisions it forces

  • The Director's veto is non-negotiable — a struck option simply never appears. The Stranger is boxed by the role chosen at creation (the traveler, the servant, the raven), not a free agent.
  • Below a quorum the Stranger sleeps — three people voting is sadder than no Stranger. The Director sees "the dark is thin tonight."
  • Who writes the ballot? Director-framed options are safe; house-proposed options are electric but need the veto and a beat more time. Ship Director-framed, earn toward proposals.
  • The purity line: votes must stay free or the House's law dies here. The defensible tie to revenue: candlelight seats may propose; the dark votes. Sell the pen-to-the-ballot, never the outcome.
VII

Speak plainly — the language law

The world can be as deep as we like; the vocabulary cannot. Storium's documented growth-killer was onboarding, and plain labels are the cheapest onboarding there is. The law has three registers: controls speak plainly — if a player must understand a word to act, it's a common word, and a first session requires zero new vocabulary. Atmosphere may whisper poetry — murmur lines and visuals can say "the dark leans in," because nothing depends on reading them. Proper nouns are budgeted — the Director, the Stranger, Ink Drops. Three coins. A new one must retire an old one. The test for every label: would a first-time visitor understand it on sight?

What this buys

  • A first-time viewer can tip, vote, and take a seat without reading anything twice — the conversion path has no vocabulary toll.
  • The poetry gets stronger, not weaker: it stops carrying instructions, so it can be pure atmosphere.
  • Terms players coin themselves ("gilded," "the dark") become community slang — earned, not assigned.

Discipline it demands

  • Every new feature will arrive with a lovely coined name attached. The name goes in the marketing copy; the button gets the plain verb.
  • The three proper nouns are a hard budget: the Director, the Stranger, Ink Drops. Adding a fourth means retiring one.
  • The mocks above have been re-labeled to this law — it applies to us first.

Recommendation

Set in Ink, performed in Live Ink

The shell is Concept I; the soul is Concept IV. Set in Ink is the only layout that physically enforces the spec — there is nowhere to put a fourteenth control, because there is nothing but a page. And its one risk, quietness, is exactly what Live Ink answers: the page is never silent because it is never finished — ink flows, dries, is struck through, is watched. "A blank page" and "a live performance" turn out to be the same design.

From Concept II, take the law, not the layout: mechanics never touch history. A roll or a vote exists only at the live edge of the page; the moment it resolves it becomes one line of set type. That single rule is what keeps the compiled chapter clean and the play surface honest — and it costs nothing to adopt inside Concept I.

From Concept III, take the one moment of theatre: when the Director sets a name down, something visibly travels — the ink of the signature line shifts, the named name flares. Presence compressed to a single breathing line of signatures, not a rail of avatars. If real tables later tell us they miss faces, seats can return — but they should have to earn their way back in.

Type is set in system approximations here; the build uses Fraunces / Literata with the Caveat hand and the existing ink palette. All three mocks run on the same fixture scene so the deltas you're seeing are concept, not content.
