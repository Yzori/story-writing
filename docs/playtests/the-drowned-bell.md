# The Drowned Bell

The rowboat's keel grinds against the tip of the old steeple, the only thing left above the black water. Below you, warped and green through six feet of tide, the bell swings — slow, deliberate, tolling a note you feel in your teeth more than hear. There is no wind, no current strong enough to move iron that heavy, and yet it rings again as you watch, sending a shudder of bubbles up past your knuckles where they grip the gunwale. On the far shore, a single lantern that was dark a moment ago is now lit, and someone standing beside it lifts a hand — not waving you in, but pointing down, at the water between you.

Vael tightens her grip on the oar and studies the figure's pointing hand, then the black water between the boat and the shore. "They want us to look, not to come," she murmurs, more to herself than to Corvin. She unclasps the small iron pendant at her throat, wraps its chain twice around her knuckles, and leans over the gunwale until her reflection breaks against the tolling of the bell — searching the green dark below for whatever the stranger means her to see.

Six feet down, the bell hangs in a haze of its own rung green light, and around its lip the water is wrong — not black like the rest of the flood but pale, as if the dark had been scraped away in a ring the width of the boat. In that cleared water Vael sees them: figures, a dozen at least, standing on the drowned street with their faces tipped up toward the surface, toward her. They are not floating. They are standing, feet on the cobbles, still as the townsfolk on a feast day, and the nearest one — a child in a Sunday collar — slowly, without a single bubble leaving its lips, opens its mouth as though to sing along with the bell.

Behind Vael the boat lurches; Corvin Ashe has shifted his weight to the gunwale, and the lantern-figure on the shore is pointing now not at the water but directly at him.

Corvin doesn't like being pointed at — never has, not since the magistrate's men. But he likes the pale scrape around that bell even less, and the child that sings without breath. He steadies himself on the gunwale and, before Vael can stop him, dips two fingers into the black water, testing whether the cold has teeth. "That fellow on the shore," he says low, not taking his eyes off the singing child below, "he's not warning us off. He's showing us his collection."

The cold does have teeth. The moment Corvin's fingers break the surface the bell's tolling changes — quickens — and every upturned face below snaps its gaze from Vael to the two fingers dipped among them, a dozen drowned heads turning as one. Something closes around Corvin's hand, not a grip but a suction, cold threading up under the skin toward the wrist, and the whole boat tips as the water begins to climb the hull in a slow, deliberate swell — the flood remembering it once had a whole town to fill. On the shore the lantern-figure finally speaks, and the voice arrives from under the water instead of across it: "Only one of you leaves with both hands. Choose now, or the bell chooses."

Corvin throws his whole weight backward, boot braced against the thwart, and hauls. The cold doesn't want to let go — it strips something as it slips, and he feels the small wet snap before he feels the pain. His hand comes free clutched to his chest, and the ring finger is simply gone below the knuckle, the wound too cold to bleed, only weeping something clear and thin. He laughs, ragged, because it's that or scream. "There. He can keep it — I was never much for wearing rings anyway. Row, Vael. Whatever he's selling, the deposit's too rich."

Vael's oars bite water but the boat won't turn — the swell has them now, sliding them not toward shore but toward the drowned steeple, toward the bell. And below, the child in the Sunday collar finally sings, and the note is a name, garbled through six feet of tide but unmistakable: it is the lantern-figure's name, called the way a congregation calls a shepherd who left the flock to drown. On the shore the man with the lantern goes to his knees. "They won't stop for a finger," he says, and his voice is breaking now, human at last. "They ring until their priest comes back down to them. That's me. It was always going to be me. But the bell has to be rung from below to fall silent — and I have never once had the courage to go under."

He holds the lantern out across the water toward the boat — an offering, or a plea. In Vael's fist the iron pendant has gone cold as the water itself, and she understands, the way clergy understand these things, exactly what he is asking her to help him do.

Vael looks at the lantern held out over the water, then at the man on his knees, and something in her goes very still — the stillness she keeps for deathbeds. "No," she says gently. "You don't need my leave to be brave, and I won't send a man to drown alone to buy his own forgiveness." She unwinds the cold pendant from her knuckles and presses it into his open palm, closing his fingers around it. "I'll go under with you. You'll ring the bell — I'll hold you to the last note and pull you up if there's breath left to save. Mercy first, penance after. Now take my hand and get in the boat."

The priest climbs into the boat like a man climbing into his own grave, and Vael goes over the side first, one hand fisted in his collar, drawing him down after her into the green. The cold takes them the way it took Corvin's finger — greedily, all at once — but she keeps her eyes open, and so does he. The drowned part for them. A dozen upturned faces, the child in the Sunday collar, they step back from the bell and make a ring, patient, waiting, the congregation that has waited ten years for a coward to find his nerve. The priest sets both hands to the rope. He rings the bell from below.

The note that comes up through the water is nothing like the tolling — it is a single, whole, human sound, the sound a bell is meant to make on the one morning it is rung right, and at the top of it every drowned face closes its mouth and its eyes and simply, finally, is still. The pale ring of cleared water goes dark. The swell drops out from under the hull all at once and the flood is just flood again, black and ordinary and quiet.

Vael breaks the surface hauling deadweight — the priest, limp, the iron pendant still crushed in his fist. Corvin gets a hand in the man's coat and together they drag him over the gunwale, and for a long moment there is only the two of them and their ragged breathing and a body that will not breathe. Then it coughs. Water, and then air. He is weeping before he is fully awake, and he does not stop, and no one asks him to.

The lantern has gone out. It doesn't matter; the moon is enough, and the water gives it back whole and unbroken all the way to the drowned steeple, where the bell hangs cold and silent and will stay that way. Corvin flexes the hand that is four fingers now and huffs something that is almost a laugh. "Cheap, in the end," he says. "One ring for a whole congregation." Vael doesn't answer. She has her hand flat on the priest's back, keeping deathbed-stillness turned inside out for once — a birth instead — and she watches the steeple until she is sure, truly sure, that nothing down there is looking back. Then she takes up the oars, and this time the boat turns, and they row for the living shore with a rescued man and a lost finger and a bell that, at last, has nothing left to say.
